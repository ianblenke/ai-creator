# Writing for the Ear: The Flow Review and the Critic
Duration: 5 - 8 min
Voice: ian. Sources and dates are in facts.md; at most three numbers per scene.

## Cold open
Here are two ways to say one thing about a made-up kettle. The first: this kettle has a thermostat, a steel base and a lid that locks. The second: this kettle switches itself off when the water boils, so you can walk away from it. The first is every fact I had. The second is the reason you would care. A script written like the first reads fine on a page, but it sounds like a list when someone hears it. This part is about catching that before you record a word.

The right card shows one kettle with a single line from the boiling water to a switch, and a small tick. A listener figure with its ears lit stands between them, leaning toward the right card.

## Intro
The first version hides its gaps on the page, where headings and layout hold it together. A listener hears no headings and sees no layout. So this part follows one idea: a listener cannot see your headings, so every change of subject, and every reason, has to be said out loud. I will show you what goes wrong when it is not, the small fix called a bridge, and the rule of one story per scene. Then come two reviewers that find those problems for you, the critic and the flow review. Last comes what neither of them can catch.

> VISUAL: A short route of five stops drawn as stepping stones: a speaker, a bridge, a single story arrow, two reviewer figures with magnifiers, a question mark. The stones light up one by one as they are named.

## A listener cannot see headings
The first stop is the heading itself. On a page, a heading tells the reader the subject has changed, and out loud there is no heading, so nothing does. So if one scene ends on the kettle's switch and the next opens on the handle, with nothing joining them, it sounds as though the narrator stopped talking and started on something else. I know that feeling from the other side: a viewer of my earlier video series told me one of my videos just stops talking and changes the subject. My rule had been that every scene opens with an opening line that ties back, but a bare question counted, and a bare question bridges nothing.

> VISUAL: A page with three scene headings down the left that fade away, leaving the text running on with a visible gap between two topics. A listener figure faces the gap with a small magenta question mark over it. Then the headings return, dim, with a cross over them.

## What a bridge is
So if a bare question bridges nothing, what does? A bridge is one or two sentences that connect two ideas and say why the next one follows. Here is one for the kettle, joining the switch to the handle. The switch means you can walk away while it boils, but you still have to come back and pour, so the next thing to look at is the handle. That is a bridge, because it gives a reason. A question is not a bridge, like: so what happens next? Neither is an announcement, like: here is the part I want to be straight about. Neither is: now let's talk about the handle. Each one points at the next topic without saying why it follows.

> VISUAL: Two topic blocks, the switch and the handle, with a plank between them labelled with the word because; the plank is lit acid green and a small figure crosses it. Beneath, three dim planks with crosses: a question mark, a megaphone, the words now let's talk.

## One story and a reason
Bridges are not enough on their own, because a script can be smooth and still be a list, like the first kettle. A scene turns into a list fastest when it carries too many numbers, and a listener cannot go back and look at a number they missed. So give each scene one story, and no more than two or three numbers, and keep the rest in your own notes. The facts you keep then have to earn their place, and a fact earns it by coming with a reason. Take the line: the base is steel. That is a bare fact, and the listener has to guess why it matters. Now add a reason: the base is steel, so it will not warp on a hot stove. The fact has become a reason. A made-up kettle is fine in an example, but in a real script a reason is only as good as the fact under it.

> VISUAL: A scene card with one story arrow through it and two small number tokens; extra tokens slide off to a notes pile at the side. Then a single bare fact tag gains a short second bar after it, lit green: the reason clause.

## The critic
Smooth bridges cannot tell you whether a claim is true, so the first reviewer, the critic, checks exactly that. The critic is an AI model that you ask to attack what the script claims. It gets three things: the script, the fact sheet, and the plan if you wrote one. The fact sheet is the list of facts, with sources, that the script is allowed to use. Every claim needs a fact behind it, because a wrong claim is the thing you cannot take back once it is public, and nothing may be stated more strongly than its source. The critic also looks for anything private, a machine, an address or a person's name, because anything said in the video is public the moment it goes out. It ends by giving the thesis as it heard it, and a verdict: ready to record, or fix first. If an AI helped you write the script, start a new conversation for the critic, because a writer cannot see its own gaps. It can only {{spoken: reed|read}}, so it reports problems instead of quietly rewriting your words.

## The flow review
But the critic reads the script as a page, and a page is exactly where my jumps hid, the ones that viewer heard. That comment is why the second reviewer exists. The critic does check how each scene opens, but you will probably miss jumps too, because you already know why one topic follows the other. So the second reviewer is the flow review: an AI model that may only {{spoken: reed|read}} your script, told to act as a listener who hears it once, from start to finish. It cannot see your headings, or the notes in the script that tell the animator what to draw. For every abrupt change it quotes the last sentence before and the first after, gives a severity of high, medium or low, and suggests a bridge of its own. It also lists terms used before they are explained, and references back to something you never said.

> VISUAL: A script page with its heading bars crossed out, passing under a listener figure with a magnifier. At each gap a small card pops out: two quoted bars, a severity chip (high, medium, low) and a short bridge bar in green. A second list of two items: a term and a back-reference.

## Running them
Both reviewers only work if they see what they should, the critic its facts, the flow review only the script. So you run each one in its own fresh folder, holding only its input files. The flow review gets no fact sheet, because a listener has none. To stop either one from changing anything, I run them in the Claude Code agent, an AI assistant that runs in a terminal, and I allow it only to {{spoken: reed|read}} files. The critic goes first, because there is no point smoothing a sentence that turns out to be wrong. Then you fix what it found, run the flow review, and fix again. Fix every high and every medium, and run the flow review once more on the new script. You do all of this before any recording, because changing a sentence on the page costs nothing, and changing it after it is recorded means recording it again.

> VISUAL: A row of four boxes in order: critic, fix, flow review, fix. A small recording-mic icon sits dim after them behind a gate bar labelled before recording. A loop arrow returns from the last fix to the flow review.

## What they cannot catch
But before you record, remember that a script that passes both reviews has still only passed a {{spoken: reeding|reading}}, because both reviewers {{spoken: reed|read}} text and neither can hear. I do not think they can tell whether a sentence sounds like you, whether a joke lands, or what you actually believe. So you {{spoken: reed|read}} the script out loud yourself once, and you keep your own opinions in it, said plainly as yours: "I think", not "it is". Reading it aloud does one more job: you hear the phrases that are hard to say. That matters if, like me, you record with a cloned copy of your own voice, which stumbles in its own ways. Its checks come in a later part.

## Close
That is the whole routine: write for the ear, bridge every change of subject, and let two reviewers check the script before you record, one that attacks and one that listens. That is the end of this part. If you will record the script in a cloned voice, as I do, you first need to know whether you are allowed to. That is the next part: the law and the licence.
