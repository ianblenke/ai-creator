# Facts: how a kettle knows when to switch off

Read on 2026-10-26. Plain-language facts, each with a source. No brand, person, place or machine names.
Mechanisms vary between kettles; facts below say "many" or "one design" where that matters.

1. Many kettles switch off with a steam-sensing bimetal: a pipe carries the steam made at the boil to a bimetallic part, which flexes and cuts the current.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_kettle (read 2026-10-26)

2. The part that senses the steam is usually a dished bimetallic disc: two bonded metals that expand by different amounts, so the disc bends as it heats and, at its switching temperature, flips ("snaps") suddenly.
   Source: https://patents.google.com/patent/US6914514 (read 2026-10-26); search summary of an engineering explainer, https://www.ingenia.org.uk/articles/how-do-kettles-know-when-to-switch-off (page itself blocked; seen only in a search snippet on 2026-10-26)

3. The snap moves a pushrod that trips an over-centre lever, which pulls the electrical contacts apart. The sudden flip gives a larger force than movement, so the contacts open sharply instead of slowly.
   Source: https://patents.google.com/patent/US6914514 (read 2026-10-26)

4. Because the disc is hit by steam, not by the water, the water temperature itself does not trip it. Steam is a fast, clear signal that the water is boiling.
   Source: engineering explainer snippet, https://www.ingenia.org.uk/articles/how-do-kettles-know-when-to-switch-off (search snippet only, 2026-10-26)

5. Little steam forms before the boil, so the trip temperature is set well below 100 degrees C. This also lets the design work at high altitude, where water boils cooler.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_kettle (read 2026-10-26)

6. Where the steam enters differs: axially or sideways onto the disc, through a longer duct, or routed through the handle or a housing.
   Source: https://patents.google.com/patent/US6914514 (read 2026-10-26)

7. After tripping, some disc types spring back by themselves once they cool about 30 degrees C below the switching temperature; others use a manual reset knob linked to the lever.
   Source: https://patents.google.com/patent/US6914514 (read 2026-10-26)

8. Boiling-dry protection is a separate part from the steam switch. A steam switch alone does not protect a kettle switched on with no water; "thermal limiters" do that.
   Source: https://patents.google.com/patent/US6914514 (read 2026-10-26)

9. One design of dry-boil protection uses a snap-acting bimetal pressed against the heating element. It cuts power when the element gets too hot, which happens when there is no water to carry the heat away.
   Source: https://patents.google.com/patent/US4539468 (read 2026-10-26)

10. That design adds a second, independent bimetal set to trip at a higher temperature. It acts only if the first fails, for example if its contacts weld shut.
    Source: https://patents.google.com/patent/US4539468 (read 2026-10-26)

11. The element can keep getting hotter for a while after the protector trips in a dry switch-on, so the trip point has to leave margin.
    Source: https://patents.google.com/patent/US4539468 (read 2026-10-26)

12. Household liquid-heating appliances, kettles included, are covered by an international safety standard (IEC 60335-2-15, current edition 2024; scope: up to 250 V, kettles up to 10 litres).
    Source: https://www.iecee.org/certification/iec-standards/iec-60335-2-152024 (seen in search results 2026-10-26; text of the standard not read)
