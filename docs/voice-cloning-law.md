# Cloning your own voice: rules, watermark, disclosure

**Not legal advice.** This page lists what the repository's author found while setting up a channel that narrates with a clone of his
own voice: which pages were read, on what day, by whom, and what they said. Nobody has given a legal opinion on any of it. Laws differ
by place and are changing; read the sources yourself on the day you publish, and get your own advice if your situation needs it.

Who read it: every row below was read by **a model-assisted research agent** (an AI agent fetching the page) on the date shown. **No
human has read these pages for this document yet.** A row's "status" says how far the reading goes, never that a use is allowed.

## What this project does, whatever the rules turn out to be
- Clone only your own voice, or one you have written permission to clone; record your consent in `voice/CONSENT.md`.
- Every description says the narration is a cloned copy of your voice and names the voice model, its licence and a link. The upload
  tool refuses a description without those two lines and sets YouTube's altered-or-synthetic flag on every upload (`docs/upload.md`).
- The recommended voice lane, Chatterbox, watermarks every output; this project never turns that off or patches it out (below).

## Own-voice cloning: what was found
- YouTube's disclosure page names cloning your own voice for voice-overs as a change that needs no disclosure (row 1). Its rules on
  impersonation still apply to anyone else's voice.
- An earlier research pass (2026-10-26, model-assisted agents, mostly law-firm summaries; not re-read for this page) found US state
  voice laws that concern using **another** person's voice without consent, and none that bars cloning your own. That is an inference
  from absence, not a finding that it is allowed.
- The voice model's own terms matter too: OmniVoice's README prohibits unauthorized voice cloning and impersonation, and its weights
  are CC-BY-NC (`docs/licences.md`).
